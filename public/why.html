Classification needs two sides of the equation

Worker classification — IR35, SDC, Scheinselbständigkeit — depends on the characteristics of the job and the worker. How they'll be managed. Where they're based. What the contractual terms look like.

At the intake stage, you only have one side: the manager's intent and a rough description of the work. You don't know who the worker is yet.

Classifying before you have the worker's details means classifying based on assumptions. If that upstream classification turns out wrong, you now have an audit trail that proves you thought about compliance and still got it wrong. That's arguably worse than not having one.

The Front Door philosophy

The compliance argument, honestly

Some platforms argue that classification should happen "at the point of origin." It sounds rigorous. Here's why it doesn't hold up.

You're classifying with assumptions

At intake, you know the work but not the worker. The manager says "6-month project, one person, deliverable-based." Looks like SOW. But if the actual worker ends up being managed day-to-day on-site, that upstream classification is wrong — and now there's documentation proving you assessed it and still got it wrong.

A compliance file isn't compliance

A timestamped PDF of an intake questionnaire looks official. But if the classification was made without knowing the worker's circumstances, jurisdiction-specific details, and actual management structure, the document records a guess — not a determination.

Determinism doesn't mean accuracy

Getting the same wrong answer every time isn't a feature. Deterministic routing from incomplete data just means you're systematically wrong in a reproducible way.

The right moment to classify

Classification belongs at engagement creation — when you know the job, the worker, the jurisdiction, the management structure, and the contractual terms. That's what Worksome does. And that's why Front Door doesn't try to do it upstream.
